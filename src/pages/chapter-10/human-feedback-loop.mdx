# 10.1 Human Feedback Loop

Suppose a new model receives more thumbs-up votes. Did it answer better, or did the product send it easier requests? A feedback log can help answer that question if it records who had a chance to vote on which answer, including the displays that drew no vote.

---

## Offline and Online Feedback

In an **offline** study, reviewers work from a rubric. They can demonstrate a response, rank candidates, critique an answer, or record a tie or an abstention. Researchers can assign the same item to several reviewers. InstructGPT used demonstrations and rankings, and Bai and colleagues collected comparisons of assistant responses. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2)

**Online** feedback reveals failures that users encounter in the product. Which answer a user sees depends on routing and display order. A user who leaves after a slow response may never vote. Record the candidate answers, which ones appeared and where, the serving policy and model revision, decoding settings, latency, errors, and whether a vote was cast. For a randomized A/B test, randomly assign eligible requests to models and log the probability of each assignment (its propensity). For ranked results, a controlled experiment can interleave the two rankings.

<a id="feedback-exposure-example"></a>

### How can one model get 80% thumbs-up and another 50%?

Suppose each model serves 100 requests. To isolate the effect of routing, assume every display gets exactly one vote, up or down. Each cell shows thumbs-up votes divided by answers displayed.

| Request type | Model A | Model B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine | 72/80 (90%) | 18/20 (90%) |
| Multi-step | 8/20 (40%) | 32/80 (40%) |
| All requests | 80/100 (80%) | 50/100 (50%) |

The models tie within each request type. Model A got 80 routine requests and B got 20, enough to create the entire 30-percentage-point gap. If the target workload is half routine and half multi-step, both estimates become `0.5 × 90% + 0.5 × 40% = 65%`. In a real log, keep missing votes separate from thumbs-down votes. Matching the request mix alone cannot tell you how users who skipped the vote would have rated the answer.

## Annotation Contract

Write a task-specific rubric with examples of correct, partially correct, unsafe, overly refusing, unverifiable, and out-of-scope answers. Let annotators mark **tie** when responses are equivalent and **abstain** when evidence or expertise is insufficient. Forcing a winner in either case adds mislabeled comparisons. Human judgments and the reward models fitted to them have distinct limitations. [[3]](#ref-3)

Calibrate annotators on a human gold set, measure **inter-annotator agreement** by domain and difficulty, and adjudicate critical disagreements. Store rubric version, randomized position, annotator qualification, timestamps, confidence, and reason codes. Monitor systematic differences across demographic, language, accessibility, and domain slices without using sensitive attributes beyond consented policy.

Privacy is part of the data contract. Define consent, retention, access, deletion, and reuse scope; detect and redact **PII**, secrets, credentials, and third-party content before annotation or training. Keep raw production logs outside the training corpus until approved transformation produces a versioned manifest.

## Active Learning Without Feedback Loops

Use the review budget on cases where a label could change a decision. Prioritize uncertain predictions, disagreements between evaluators, novel requests, shifts in traffic, and errors with serious consequences. Also sample ordinary requests at a known probability.

Record each request’s probability of selection for review, separately from its probability of assignment to a model. To estimate quality across all traffic, weight the results from each request type by its share of traffic, or account for unequal review probabilities when sampling individual requests. A raw average of the hard cases answers a different question.

For verifiable tasks, pair human preference with executable evidence: unit tests, tool-state checks, citations, or environment outcomes. A reward model should not overrule a failed deterministic verifier merely because the explanation is fluent.

## The Badcase Loop Without Leakage

An incident is useful for regression testing, but a sibling of a training example is not an independent generalization test.

1. Freeze prompt, context, tool state, expected behavior, artifact revision and failure taxonomy.
2. Assign the incident and all paraphrases, related conversations and synthetic siblings to one semantic cluster before splitting.
3. Either quarantine the entire cluster for evaluation, or admit it to the training candidate and explicitly mark its original and sibling regression tests as exposed.
4. Keep release evaluation in separate untouched clusters; never transfer their prompts, rubrics or teacher-generated variants into training.
5. Report known-incident regression and held-out-cluster generalization separately, alongside capability retention and safety.

Passing an exposed regression test shows that a known failure was addressed. Only a separate, protected evaluation can support a claim about generalization.

---

## Admission, Training, and Release Gates

Define separate thresholds for data admission and model release. For data, track source yield, ties/abstentions, agreement, judge disagreement, PII rejection, and slice coverage. For training, pin the manifest, split clusters, reference/baseline artifact, tokenizer/templates, objective, and seeds. Save optimizer/scheduler/scaler/RNG/sampler/data cursor with the checkpoint.

For release, use fixed decoding and paired comparisons with uncertainty. Gate target quality, base retention, safety/over-refusal, format/tool correctness, latency, cost, and error rate; critical slice failures cannot be averaged away. Shadow, canary, and ramp-up stages need automatic abort windows and a rehearsed last-known-good rollback.

## Quizzes

<details>
<summary>Quiz 1: Why is a production thumbs-up not an unbiased quality label?</summary>
*Routing determines which requests a model sees. Placement and latency can affect who votes. In the example, A receives more routine requests, which explains its 30-percentage-point lead even though the models tie on both request types.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 2: Why allow ties and abstentions?</summary>
*Equivalent answers have no honest direction, and some comparisons exceed the annotator's evidence or expertise. Forcing a winner converts uncertainty into systematic label noise.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 3: Why is a sibling of a training incident not an independent holdout?</summary>
*Related examples share information and must be clustered before splitting. Quarantine the whole cluster, or label its regression tests as exposed if it enters training. Evaluate generalization on separate untouched clusters.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 4: Why retain random traffic alongside active-learning samples?</summary>
*Reviewers who see mostly uncertain cases can identify failures but cannot estimate typical performance from the raw average. A random sample of ordinary requests, with known sampling probabilities and attention to missing labels, helps test whether gains reach the users who make those requests.*
</details>
