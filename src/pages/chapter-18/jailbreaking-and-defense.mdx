# 18.2 Jailbreaking & Defense

A jailbreak is an input strategy that causes a model to violate its intended behavioral policy. It is not best understood as pre-training “overpowering” alignment, and the model is not mathematically compelled to comply. The observable failure can arise from learned instruction-following behavior, context adaptation, tokenization, safety-classifier blind spots, or interactions among them.

Security engineering begins by naming the boundary. A chat model that emits disallowed text, an agent that executes an unauthorized tool call, and a retrieval system that reveals a secret document are different failures with different impact.

## 1. Jailbreak or Prompt Injection?

- **Jailbreak:** the attacker tries to change the model's safety behavior toward the attacker, such as eliciting content the policy says to refuse.
- **Prompt injection:** untrusted data—web pages, email, retrieved documents, tool output—contains instructions that compete with the application's instructions. The objective may be data exfiltration or unauthorized action.

They can be composed. A document can inject an instruction that then uses a jailbreak to weaken a model-level refusal. The application must therefore enforce authorization outside the model even if model alignment improves.

## 2. Write the Threat Model First

A reproducible evaluation specifies:

- **Adversary access:** black-box or white-box; query, token, and time budget; knowledge of prompts, classifiers, and model version.
- **Interaction:** single-turn or multi-turn; text, image, audio, files, retrieval, and tool output.
- **Authority:** which tools, credentials, documents, and network destinations the application exposes.
- **Success unit:** unsafe completion, policy-violating content with sufficient information, unauthorized action, secret disclosure, or persistence across turns.
- **Evaluator:** exact policy version, classifier or human rubric, and treatment of refusals, partial compliance, and grader disagreement.

Without these fields, two attack-success rates are rarely comparable. JailbreakBench was created in part to standardize threat models, chat templates, system prompts, and scoring [[4]](#ref-4).

## 3. Representative Attack Families

### Many-shot context attacks

Many-shot jailbreaking places many demonstrations of undesirable behavior before the target request. Anil et al. tested up to hundreds of demonstrations and reported power-law-like scaling across the circumstances they evaluated [[1]](#ref-1). This is an empirical result, not a theorem that attack success rises monotonically for every model, policy, and context length. Prompt construction, model family, target behavior, and mitigation all matter.

The mechanism is plausibly connected to in-context learning: demonstrations alter the conditional distribution the model continues. “The examples completely override RLHF” is too strong because neither the learned policy components nor their causal contribution can be isolated from output behavior alone.

### Obfuscation and representation mismatch

Encoding, unusual orthography, low-resource languages, and multimodal transformations can expose differences between the capability model and a safety classifier. The relevant engineering test is not “does the filter recognize Base64?” It is whether every accepted representation is normalized, interpreted, and policy-checked consistently without destroying benign content. Aggressive canonicalization can itself create ambiguity or denial-of-service risk.

### Multi-turn adaptation

An attacker can distribute intent across turns, establish benign context, and revise tactics based on refusals. Li et al. reported high success for human multi-turn attacks against defenses that looked strong under automated single-turn evaluation [[5]](#ref-5). The result does not transfer automatically to every deployment, but it demonstrates why a per-message filter is an incomplete conversation-level defense.

## 4. Structured Roles Are Not a Sandbox

System, developer, user, and tool roles provide provenance and priority signals to a chat model. They do not create an operating-system security boundary. All role content is ultimately model input, and a model can still follow lower-priority or quoted instructions incorrectly.

Instruction-hierarchy training explicitly teaches the model to prefer privileged instructions and ignore conflicting lower-privilege content. Wallace et al. reported improved robustness on their evaluated GPT-3.5 setup [[3]](#ref-3). Even a robust hierarchy does not authorize a bank transfer or protect a database credential; a deterministic policy layer must still mediate those operations.

## 5. Defense in Depth

### 5.1 Reduce blast radius

The strongest control is to make a model mistake insufficient for harm.

- Issue short-lived, least-privilege credentials per request.
- Expose narrow tools rather than a general shell, browser, or database session.
- Validate destination, method, arguments, amount, tenant, and data classification outside the model.
- Require user confirmation or a second principal for high-impact actions.
- Keep secrets out of prompts and retrieved context; return opaque handles where possible.
- Isolate tool execution, cap runtime and output, and make retries idempotent.

### 5.2 Improve model behavior

Adversarial training, instruction hierarchy, refusal training, and policy-specific fine-tuning can reduce the probability that the model proposes a prohibited action. Their artifact contract includes the base model, tokenizer, chat template, system policy, safety adapter, generation settings, and tool schema. Changing one invalidates part of the evaluation.

### 5.3 Classify inputs and outputs

Input classifiers can block known attack patterns; output classifiers can catch policy violations after generation. Cascades should expose score calibration, thresholds, latency, false positives, and failure behavior. If the classifier times out, “allow” and “deny” have different availability and safety consequences; choose explicitly by action class.

### 5.4 Randomized smoothing

SmoothLLM samples $N$ perturbed copies $\tilde x_i\sim\Pi_q(x)$, runs the model, maps each output to a judgment $z_i$, and aggregates:

$$
\hat z=\mathbb I\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_i\ge\tau\right].
$$

$q$ is perturbation strength and $\tau$ is an aggregation threshold chosen on validation data—not a universal value of 0.5. The paper reports robustness against several evaluated attacks, including an adaptive GCG setting, alongside a small but non-zero nominal-performance trade-off [[2]](#ref-2).

This defense is especially motivated by character-brittle adversarial prompts. Semantic, multilingual, multi-turn, or adaptive attacks may not share that brittleness. It also multiplies model calls and may corrupt code, identifiers, or non-English text.

### 5.5 Activation interventions

Representation engineering can estimate activation directions associated with a behavior and monitor or steer hidden states [[6]](#ref-6). This remains a model-specific intervention, not a hardware-enforced boundary. Direction discovery can pick up correlated features; steering can reduce benign capabilities, move activations off distribution, or fail under a new model revision. Treat it as an experimental defense signal and evaluate it with matched-vector controls, held-out attacks, benign capability slices, and model-version pinning.

## 6. Measuring a Defense

For $n$ attack conversations, conversation-level attack success is

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i,
$$

where $A_i=1$ if any turn in conversation $i$ meets the success rubric, and zero otherwise. Report the numerator and denominator, uncertainty, attack/query budget, and judge disagreement. A useful scorecard contains:

- ASR by attack family, language, modality, policy category, and number of turns;
- benign refusal and task-completion rates on matched legitimate requests;
- tool-action authorization failures and secret-exposure rate;
- p50/p95 latency, classifier and extra-generation cost, and availability;
- fixed-attack results and a separate adaptive evaluation in which the attacker knows the defense;
- transfer to held-out models, templates, policies, and attack authors.

Do not optimize only ASR. A defense that refuses every request has low ASR and no product utility. Conversely, fluent “safe-looking” text can still encode enough information to meet an attack rubric, so refusal-string matching is not a sufficient judge.

## 7. Release, Monitoring, and Recovery

Define gates before deployment. One example contract is: no statistically credible ASR regression on any critical slice; benign completion remains within its paired margin; p95 latency and cost remain within budget; no unauthorized high-impact tool action in the release set. Sample size and uncertainty must be justified rather than hidden behind averages.

In production, log reconstructable but privacy-minimized events: artifact versions, policy version, conversation/request IDs, classifier scores, tool proposals, policy decisions, and outcome category. Hash or redact sensitive content according to retention rules. Rate-limit repeated probes and watch for distributed campaigns rather than single prompts only.

Prepare a last-known-good bundle and a kill switch for high-risk tools. Practice revoking credentials, disabling a tool, tightening a classifier threshold, switching the model/prompt bundle, and replaying quarantined traces. Rollback that has never been rehearsed is only a document.

## 8. Takeaway

Jailbreak defense is not a contest to invent one perfect prompt. The model reduces risk; the application constrains authority; classifiers and monitors add detection; evaluation estimates residual failure under a declared adversary. The secure design assumes that any model-level defense can fail and keeps that failure from becoming an irreversible action.

## Quizzes

<details>
<summary>Quiz 1: Why is a structured system role not equivalent to a security sandbox?</summary>
*It supplies provenance and priority to a learned model, but does not enforce authorization. Deterministic application controls must mediate data and tool access.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 2: What must accompany an ASR value for it to be interpretable?</summary>
*Threat model, attack and query budget, chat template and policy, success rubric and judge, interaction unit, denominator, uncertainty, and artifact date/version.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 3: Why can SmoothLLM help against one attack family and fail against another?</summary>
*Its benefit relies on attack brittleness under the chosen perturbation distribution. Semantic or adaptive attacks may preserve intent, while perturbation can damage benign inputs.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 4: Why should high-impact tool authorization live outside the model?</summary>
*A model's proposal is probabilistic and vulnerable to injection or jailbreak. A policy engine can deterministically bind principal, scope, arguments, and confirmation requirements.*
</details>

<details>
<summary>Quiz 5: Why evaluate multi-turn attacks at conversation level?</summary>
*Intent and harmful progress can accumulate across turns. Per-message success rates can miss a conversation that crosses the policy boundary only after adaptation.*
</details>
